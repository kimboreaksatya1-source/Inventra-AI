The Design Thesis — Win the Room in 30 Seconds

The judge starts the demo with a mental model: “another inventory tool.” Our entire job in the first 30 seconds is to break it.

Every POS vendor demo opens the same way: a product list, a stock count, a sales chart. If we open like that, we have already lost — we look like a cheaper version of something the judge has seen ten times. So we do the opposite. We do not show data. We show a decision.

The user drops a messy spreadsheet onto the screen, and a few seconds later Inventra AI hands back a one-page briefing written like a consultant’s memo: a plain-language verdict, the three things that need attention this week, and a ranked action plan — each action carrying a dollar figure. Then a chat box: “Ask me anything about your business.”

The three things a judge must feel

1 · It read our real file.
Not a clean template — our actual export, with blank cells and mixed Khmer/English names.
2 · It has an opinion.
Not charts to interpret — a written verdict and a recommendation.
3 · It counted the money.
Every risk and opportunity is quantified: dollars at stake, dollars to gain.

What we deliberately do not show first

Stock levels. Product tables. Reports. Reorder points. These are table stakes — necessary in the product, fatal as the opening move. They can appear later, once the judge has re-categorised us.

The 30-second test

If, after the demo, a judge cannot turn to the person next to them and say “it read their data and told them what to do — and what it was worth”, the demo failed. Every screen below is engineered to produce exactly that sentence.

Hero Demo Scenario

One file. Thirty seconds. A deliberate, rehearsed sequence — and it also runs live on a judge’s own data.

The file. shop_march.xlsx — one sheet, 3,412 rows, 92 days of sales, product names in mixed Khmer and English, a scatter of blank cells, no tidy header formatting. Chosen to look exactly like what a real mini-mart owner would export and email to themselves.

0:00Presenter drags shop_march.xlsx onto a near-empty screen. One line of copy: “Drop your sales export.”“Okay, an import screen. Standard.”
0:03“Reading your business…” Counters animate: 3,412 rows · 287 products · 92 days · 6 categories. Column mapping appears — Product, Qty, Unit price, Date — each with a ✓ and a confidence score.“It figured out my messy columns by itself. It didn’t make me map anything.”
0:08The screen dissolves into The Business Brief. A headline verdict in plain language. Health score 72/100 (▲4). Subline: “3 things need you this week.”“Wait — this is a written briefing. Not a dashboard.”

The live variant

The scripted run builds confidence; the live run on a judge’s own spreadsheet wins it. Same pipeline, same 8 seconds. Offer it every time — the willingness to run unseen data is itself the proof.

The AI Business Brief

The core artifact. A one-page briefing that reads like a sharp analyst wrote it overnight — not a dashboard, a document.

The Brief is what makes Inventra feel like a person rather than a panel of charts. It is generated on upload and refreshed every morning. It is scannable in 60 seconds and it always ends in three actions or fewer.

Figure 3.1 — Anatomy of the Brief. Verdict first; actions last; never more than one screen.

3.1 The eight blocks

BlockWhat it contains
The Verdict2–3 sentences in plain language. The single most important paragraph — what’s going well, what’s at risk, what to do about it.
Health ScoreA 0–100 composite of revenue trend, margin, inventory efficiency, cash position, and concentration — shown with its weekly delta and the one factor moving it most.
Business HealthFour tiles — Revenue, Margin, Inventory efficiency, Cash position — each with direction and a one-line “why.”
The Money MapA single bar showing where cash is right now: healthy stock on the shelf, capital trapped in dead stock, cash available in the bank.
RisksRanked, five max, each with a severity (🔴 today / 🟠 this week / 🟡 watch) and a time-to-impact.
OpportunitiesRanked, five max, each with an upside estimate in dollars per week.
Today’s ActionsThree max, one-tap, each carrying its projected impact. This is the part the owner acts on.
Ask InventraA chat box grounded in the same computed numbers — no new figures invented in conversation.

AI Risk Detection

Detect the risks a busy owner cannot see — because they are spread across time, categories, and systems that never talk to each other.

An owner standing behind the till sees today. They do not see a category that has declined for three weeks straight, a supplier whose lead time has quietly crept from two days to five, or the fact that next week’s reorder plan is larger than the cash in the drawer. Inventra reads across all of it and ranks what matters.

Inventory risk

RiskSignal Inventra readsExample alert
Imminent stockout on an A-itemVelocity × days-of-cover falls below lead time + safety buffer“Coca-Cola 1.25L — 4 days left, delivery takes 2. Reorder today.”
Silent stockoutOn-hand at 0 for >72h while the SKU is still “active” and had steady sales“Nescafé 3-in-1 has been out for 5 days — you’re losing ~$12/day and may not have noticed.”
Expiry-bound overstockCover > shelf-life remaining, for perishables“You have 60 days of yogurt cover and it expires in 21. Promote or return 40 units.”
Dead stockZero sales for 45+ days with capital > threshold“6 SKUs, $430, no sale since January. Clear them.”
Feast-or-famine SKU on a fixed reorder pointHigh demand variance managed by a static rule“Ice cream swings 5× week to week — your fixed reorder causes both stockouts and spoilage.”
Shrinkage signalUnits sold consistently < units depleted from stock“Cigarettes: 18 units left the shelf, 14 were rung up, 4 weeks running. Investigate.”

Revenue risk

RiskSignalExample alert
Revenue concentrationTop 5 SKUs or top 2 days > ~60% of revenue“68% of your revenue is 5 products. If one supplier fails, most of your income does too.”
Shrinking basketAverage items-per-transaction trending down 3+ weeks“Baskets dropped from 3.1 to 2.6 items. Customers are buying less per visit — check placement and bundles.”
Quietly declining categoryCategory revenue down each week for 3 weeks, masked by growth elsewhere“Household goods have fallen 4 weeks running — hidden because beverages grew.”
Underpriced fast-moverVery high velocity + margin below category norm“Iced coffee sells out daily at 23% margin vs 31% category. You’re leaving ~$25/wk on the table.”
Recurring day-part selloutSame SKUs hit 0 at the same time each day/week“You run out of bread every Friday by 2pm — ~$40 of demand walks out unserved.”

Supplier risk

RiskSignalExample alert
Single-supplier dependencyOne supplier > 50% of purchase value or of revenue-linked SKUs“Distributor A supplies 68% of your revenue. Add a second source for beverages.”
Creeping lead timeOrder-to-delivery gap increasing order over order“Distributor A now takes 5 days, not 2. Your safety stock is set for 2 — raise it or switch.”
Price driftOne supplier’s unit costs rising faster than others for comparable goods“Your snack supplier’s prices are up 9% in 2 months; the market is up 3%.”
Falling fill rateDelivered quantity < ordered quantity, trend“Last 4 orders arrived 12% short. You’re planning around stock you never receive.”
MOQ-forced overstockMinimum order quantity > sensible cover for the SKU“The 48-unit case gives you 70 days of cover on a slow item — split the order or drop the SKU.”

Cash flow risk

RiskSignalExample alert
Rising trapped-capital ratioShare of stock value in slow/non-moving SKUs increasing“22% of your stock value is now slow-moving, up from 14%. Cash is freezing on the shelves.”
Reorder exceeds cashRecommended purchase plan > cash on hand“This week’s ideal reorder is $780; you have $610. Here’s the $610 version that protects your top sellers.”
Predicted shortfall on a dateProjected outflows > inflows on a known day“You’ll be about $180 short on the 15th when the supplier invoice is due. Delay the snack reorder to bridge it.”
Margin compression trendBlended margin declining while revenue flat/up“You’re selling more but keeping less — margin down 1.4 pts over a month, mostly discounting.”
Seasonal cash crunch aheadHistorical pattern of a demand spike needing stock 3 weeks out“Khmer New Year needs ~$900 of extra stock in 3 weeks. Start setting aside $300/wk now.”

The same data that shows what’s wrong also shows what’s possible. Inventra ranks the upside by dollars-per-week and effort.

OpportunityPattern Inventra readsExample & estimated upside
Accelerating productVelocity rising and the rate of rise is increasing, while stock cover is thin“Iced coffee +34% MoM, 5 days cover — raise the order 40%.”  +$40/wk
Emerging categoryNew or minor SKUs steadily taking basket share“Ready-meals went from 2% to 7% of baskets in 6 weeks — widen the range.”  +$60/wk

AI Action Plan

Reporting is not the product. Every insight resolves into a specific, costed action the owner can take this week.

A risk without an action is an anxiety. An opportunity without an action is a regret. Inventra never leaves an insight hanging — each one carries a recommended move, the reasoning, a projected dollar impact, and an effort tag.

SituationRecommended actionRationale & projected impact
Snacks over-stocked, beverages turning fast“Reduce snack purchases by 20% and move that ~$60/wk into beverages.”Beverages turn 3× faster; snacks carry 18 days of cover. +$45/wk margin, shelf freed.
Top seller 4 days from stockout“Reorder Coca-Cola 1.25L today — 24 units — not Thursday.”Delivery takes 2 days; stockout in 4. Avoids ~$70 of lost sales.
$430 in six products, no sale since January“Mark those 6 SKUs down 30% this week; stop restocking them.”47–91 days idle. Recovers ~$300 cash in ~10 days; permanent shelf back.
Iced coffee: high velocity, low elasticity“Raise iced-coffee price by $0.10.”Volume held through the last two price moves. +$22/wk margin.
Water + noodles: 61% attach, not merchandised“Create a water + instant-noodle bundle at $1.90; shelve them together.”Already bought together most of the time. +8% attach, ~+$30/wk.
Friday demand exceeds one delivery“Split the weekly delivery into Wednesday + Friday.”You sell out Friday afternoons. Captures ~$110/wk of unmet demand.
68% of revenue from one supplier“Onboard a second beverage supplier for your top 5 SKUs.”Removes a single point of failure. ~5% price leverage + resilience.

Differentiation — “I Already Have a POS. Why Inventra?”

Because a POS is a system of record. Inventra is a system of decisions. They are not the same category.

Your POS is your business’s memory. Inventra AI is its judgment. One records what happened. The other reads across all of it and tells you what to do next — and what it’s worth.

Inventra does not replace the POS. It sits on top of it — and on top of Excel, supplier messages, and anything else the business already produces. Keep the POS. Add a brain.

DimensionPOS / inventory systemInventra AI
Core jobRecord transactions and stockRead across all the data and decide what to do
Question it answers“What did I sell?”“What should I do next — and why?”
OutputReports and screens you interpretA briefing written for you, plus a ranked action plan
Time orientationBackward — what already happenedForward — predictive and prescriptive
AlertsReactive thresholds (“low stock”)Risk detected early, ranked by money and time-to-impact
Scope of viewOne store’s transactionsSales + margin + cash + suppliers + seasonality, together
Who does the analysisYou do — the POS needs an analyst to be usefulInventra is the analyst
InterfaceMenus and tablesA one-page brief and a chat box

Three reasons an owner turns Inventra on even with a perfect POS

  1. The POS never tells them what to do. It shows the numbers and stops. The owner still has to be the analyst at 10pm.
  2. The POS only sees transactions. It cannot connect a supplier’s slipping lead time to next month’s cash gap to the category that’s quietly declining. Inventra holds all of that in one view.
  3. The POS makes them work for the insight. Inventra delivers the insight — and the action, and the dollar figure — before they’ve finished their coffee.

Future Vision — From Inventory Intelligence to Business Autopilot

Four stages. At each one Inventra sees more, does more, and asks the owner for less — but the owner always sets the goals.

Figure 8.1 — The evolution ladder. Same product, widening authority.
StageQuestion it answersWhat it can seeWhat it doesThe owner’s role
1 — Inventory Intelligence (MVP)“What’s happening on my shelves?”Sales history and stock levelsForecasts demand, flags dead stock and stockouts, suggests reordersMakes every buying decision
2 — Business Intelligence“How is my business really doing?”+ margin, cash position, suppliers, seasonalityWrites the daily Brief; detects risk and opportunity across the whole business; costs every actionDecides, using the Brief
3 — Operating Copilot“What should I do — and can you help me do it?”+ pricing, promotions, supplier catalogues, staffingConversational and proactive; drafts purchase orders, price changes and promotions for one-tap approvalApproves; sets strategy
4 — Business Autopilot“Run the routine; flag the exceptions.”+ bank feed, e-commerce, multi-branchExecutes routine decisions inside owner-set budgets and guardrails — auto-reorder, dynamic pricing, supplier switching — and surfaces only exceptionsSets goals and guardrails; handles exceptions
The through-line

We are not building software that manages inventory. We are building the operating system that runs the business — starting with the one decision an SME owner makes every single week: what to buy.

How the Wow Actually Works

The magic is a pipeline. Deterministic maths does the analysis; the language model writes the story — it never invents a number.

Figure A.1 — The pipeline. Judges can trust the figures because a language model never computes them.

In scope for build week

File ingest + auto-mapping · retail-stats engine · ~8 risk detectors · ~6 opportunity detectors · Brief generator · grounded chat · action list with projected impact · the 30-second demo flow.

Deliberately out of scope (for now)

Live POS integration · write-back (auto-drafting real POs) · multi-branch consolidation · bank-feed cash forecasting · autonomous execution. These are Stages 3–4, not the MVP.

Do not build another inventory system. Build the analyst that reads one — and, eventually, the operating system that runs the shop.
